## The Logic of Irregularity

There are several characteristics of irregular warfare (IW) that produce its inherent level of complexity:

* **IW is dominated by an asymmetry of consequences.** The stakes of the conflict are always existential to the insurgent, but are relatively peripheral to the counterinsurgent. This asymmetry has a tendency to compensate for subsequent asymmetries of scale in force size, training, or matériel. 
* **IW cannot be waged with conventional military strategy.** A group of French officers writing under the pseudonym <em>“Ximenes”</em> noted of IW that it is not “for the control of territory or military objectives, nor even so much for the destruction of the opposing force, but for the conquest of the population” [1]. 

Given this population-centricity, it follows that political methods, rather than military ones, are central to securing political goals. This very logic can also render military operations self-defeating when unaccompanied by robust socio-economic programs, propaganda, police measures, and intelligence collection. To competently face their adversary, the counterinsurgent must be willing and prepared to act with a total unity of military and civil efforts. This unity, as one might imagine, blurs the lines between the army, the state, and the society itself. The scope of the war cannot be held to the hinterlands and border areas, but extends to encompass the totality of the area where the population resides. By pure virtue of being affected by the conflict, all actors become implicit participants. Even inaction on the part of civilians becomes equivalent to picking a side. Thus, both factions, by persuasion, coercion, or both, must force the population to act in their favor. This participation, even if unwilling, renders the normally uninvolved as “legitimate” targets. Since there can be no neutral parties, the war becomes, by definition, total. And like any form of total war, waging it to completion requires the state to partition broad swaths of civil responsibility to the military.

* **Lastly, IW is by precedent, if not necessarily definition, limited.** Taken at face value, this may seem like a contradiction of the prior assertion of totality but this is not the case. The limits here are not ones of <u>scope</u> but of <u>method</u>. 

IW can be thought of as total because it is all-encompassing, but it can also be thought of as limited because it is characterized by *indirect strategy* [2]. At the highest level of abstraction, victory in warfare occurs when an actor produces the desired psychological effect on the enemy. Military force is simply one of many methods to achieve this effect. While organized violence is generally central to waging IW, it is always used in conjunction with political, economic, and even diplomatic means. Rather than targeting the adversary’s conventional military forces, this combination of means has the aim of progressively disintegrating their will to resist. Notably, these indirect methods can have a particularly subversive effect when contextualized by the totality of their scope. This is because, to the insurgent, the conflict is over a population who have become valid targets themselves. By contrast, the state actor is constrained by a web of political, psychological, diplomatic, legal, and even normative constraints which raises the barrier to their taking the same view.

As a whole, these complexities reinforce the dominance of political over military considerations in unconventional conflicts. Even more importantly, they highlight that the military annihilation of an insurgent formation does not implicitly grant some measure of victory over an insurgency. Instead, the annihilation of insurgent groups only has meaning insofar as it can be translated into (i.e. used to enable) political victories. For this “translation” to occur, the counterinsurgent must (1) diagnose the political basis of civil unrest, (2) break adversarial control over contaminated population by excising the insurgent’s “Political Administrative Organization" [*Organization Politico-Administrative*; OPA], and (3) cauterize the political wound. It is a direct consequence of the conflict’s totality that both of these elements, political and military success, are rendered necessary, and not independently sufficient.

With this set of problems in mind, the French General Jacques Hogard asserted that to successfully prosecute an irregular war, “[a]ll rebel territory should, as quickly as possible, be isolated from the exterior materially and morally.” [3] By restricting the movement of the rebel groups, you remove the proverbial fish from the water it inhabits. The utility of this separation is twofold: it degrades the fighting capability of the insurgent while simultaneously preparing the civilian population for pacification. This theory is sound in principle, however two practical questions remain:

<u>Question 1:</u> What is the insurgent being isolated from?

<u>Question 2:</u> How should this "isolation" be implemented?

### **Answer 1: Active Sanctuaries**

To answer the first question, it is necessary to further examine the phenomenon of “active sanctuaries” [4]. As discussed, irregular conflicts contain several complications which metastasize into a threat more severe than any of its component parts. A significant aspect of this is that they are rarely an **intra**national affair. They often involve transnational groups, grow into proxy conflicts, or simply become internationalized to the point where external actors feel compelled to intervene. In the specific context of IW, the term “active sanctuary” refers to the rebel-friendly territories within neighboring states. The exact degree of support can differ. At times, the state housing the sanctuary actively arms and supports the rebels, while at other times, it simply turns a blind eye. These sanctuaries are also geographically contiguous with the rebel zone of activity. This enables the insurgent to use them for logistics, training facilities, and command and control (C2) centers.

Almost as a rule, the states operating as sanctuaries maintain their ostensible neutrality in the conflict. This “neutrality” grants them a certain measure of plausible deniability in turn. Since the barrier for action is increased drastically by the precedent and law of international sovereignty, the rebel gains freedom of action far greater than if they were operating within the zone of insurgency alone. This boon, that of maximizing freedom of action while paralyzing the adversary, makes the transfer of insurgent men, matériel, or functions into an active sanctuary a textbook example of André Beaufre’s “external maneuver” [5].

To counter this maneuver, the counterinsurgent has two choices, both of which come with associated drawbacks. Firstly, they can collect the intelligence required to satisfy the burden of proof before taking diplomatic, economic, and legal action. Granted, this course is a lengthy process which, even if successful, has no guarantee of effectiveness. While the counterinsurgent investigates the situation and prepares litigation, the malignant insurgency will continue to spread. A more expedient alternative then, might be to accept the consequences of a military response to the sanctuary-state. Namely, diplomatic or economic isolation due to perceived aggression. However, the unfortunate fact remains that even if the counterinsurgent is willing to take military action, the sanctuary may have the power, alliances, or economic interdependence necessary to dissuade a direct confrontation. In these cases, the military response to the sanctuary may also come in a form with plausible deniability.

When a counterinsurgent has decided to use military force against an active sanctuary but cannot overcome the associated barriers, they often forgo their standing regular forces in favor of foreign or nonstate proxies. This form of conflict is referred to as “surrogate warfare” [6]. Under this scheme the aforementioned surrogates have varying levels of independence. Depending on the situation, the surrogates may or may not be conventional in nature. Nevertheless, the role of the surrogate force is to act as stand-ins for the counterinsurgent to operate where they cannot. Most notably, since they offer plausible deniability, the surrogate can operate in a way to hamper the insurgent's external maneuver.

### **Answer 2: Interdiction**

If the insurgent must be isolated from an active sanctuary, the specific method of doing this falls under the task of Interdiction. As defined in the U.S. Department of Defense’s Dictionary of Military Terms, Interdiction is:

> **1.** An action to divert, disrupt, delay, or destroy the enemy’s military surface capability before it can be used effectively against friendly forces, or to otherwise achieve objectives.

> **2.** In support of law enforcement, activities conducted to divert, disrupt, delay, intercept, board, detain, or destroy, under lawful authority, vessels, vehicles, aircraft, people, cargo, and money [7].

More generally in the context of counterinsurgency (COIN), interdiction is synonymous with undermining the insurgent’s ability to use his forces at the time and place of his choosing. By doing so, an actor can undermine the adversary’s momentum and create opportunities to target his centers of gravity. Conspicuously however, the creation of this opportunity does not require the annihilation of the adversary’s forces [8].

* The counterinsurgent can interdict by **diversion**, forcing the adversary to use his assets in ways which are disadvantageous to him.
* The counterinsurgent can interdict by **disruption**, targeting centers of gravity (e.g. C2, political cohesion, morale, fund collection, intelligence capabilities, supply lines) to undermine the execution of strategy.
* The counterinsurgent can interdict by **delaying**, introducing physical, logistical, or military barriers to negatively alter the timetable of adversary forces.
* Lastly, the counterinsurgent can interdict by **destruction** (or, ‘annihilation’), permanently harming the “structure, function, or condition of a target” in order to render it ineffective.

### **Bush Wars**

The list of insurgencies which were shaped by the presence of an external sanctuary is simply too long to include in full. However for the modern world, some of the most formative of these were those which occurred during the period of decolonization in Africa. In these conflicts, apartheid states fought against hostile non-state actors operating within their borders. But frequently, internal security requirements demanded isolating insurgent forces from external sanctuaries. This was a task which necessitated frequent cross-border raids and external counter-guerilla operations.

From 1966 to 1990, the Republic of South Africa fought a number of insurgent groups within the province of South West Africa (present-day Namibia) [9]. This effort contained many overlapping operations, which are grouped into the South African Border War, or more colloquially, the Angolan Bush War. As it progressed, the South African Defense Force (SADF) found itself focused increasingly on interdicting groups which filtered into Namibia through the Angolan and Zambian frontier. Mainly, they sought to accomplish this by manipulating the Angolan Civil War to their advantage. This was done with pseudo-operations and the use of surrogate forces (such as UNITA or the 32 Battalion) which intervened in the external conflict to create buffer zones along the border [10].

Like South Africa, from 1965 to 1980, the Republic of Rhodesia (present-day Zimbabwe) prosecuted a war of COIN against anti-apartheid rebels. The early operations of this conflict had a generally internal focus, yet the intensification of hostilities gave way to an increasing focus on external raids. These raids aimed to destroy bases and staging points located in Mozambique, Zambia, and to a lesser degree, Botswana [11]. A key difference, however, between the Rhodesian Security Forces (RSF) and SADF was that the Rhodesians lacked an overarching strategy to deal with the insurgency. By treating COIN as a war of annihilation, they were dealing with the insurgency as if it was a military, rather than political, problem. Instead of addressing the underlying political basis of the conflict (white-minority rule and the associated lack of legitimacy), the RSF set out to simply eliminate as many insurgent camps as could be identified.

Considering this crippling failure in strategic thinking, as well as their relatively smaller white population and economic base, it is not particularly surprising that the Rhodesians failed to last as long as their southern neighbors. While the Republic of South Africa did approach COIN with a focus on interdiction, their protracted campaign also failed to address the political foundations of resistance. Thus their strategy, while more successful, only served to prolong what was basically inevitable. Over the course of their conflicts, both apartheid regimes achieved stunning tactical victories but underwent political collapse. What ultimately rendered their victories Pyrrhic was a combination of mounting political pressures, both from within and without.

The standard approach to COIN in a modern professional military emphasizes the importance of a whole-of-government approach. The bush war use of interdiction flew directly in the face of this axiom. By attacking the military potential of their adversaries without undermining their political foundations, the apartheid states created new enemies with each victory. In effect, they gambled their prospects of long-term victory on a competition of attrition. The incoherence of this gamble is seen mainly in their diplomatic isolation, small “active” population, and the economic toll of war [12], which left them unable to meaningfully absorb losses.

### **Strategic Parallels**

The bush wars of sub-Saharan Africa clearly demonstrate a number of different interdiction strategies, however, these conflicts were both preceded and eclipsed by the French experience of counterinsurgency in Algeria. This would serve not just as a template for the later sub-Saharan conflicts, but as an unheeded warning. Making even a cursory survey of the bush wars reveals a marked resemblance to the political context of the Algerian War. By the start of decolonization in Africa during the Cold War, the state apparatus of South Africa and Rhodesia were dominated by a white minority which peaked at about 19% (South Africa) and 5% (Rhodesia). In the same vein, the French departments of Algeria (l’Algérie) were politically and economically dominated by European settlers [13] who were overwhelmingly French/Pied-Noir [14]. By 1960, this settler population in Algeria numbered somewhere around 1.2 million (10–11% of the total population). Another resemblance is that both the South African apartheid-states and French Algeria were suffering from a serious crisis of legitimacy following the Second World War. This crisis fed directly into the native populations developing a national consciousness separate from their colonial governments, a sense of political assertiveness, and a generally recalcitrant attitude. This newfound political energy was being actively harnessed and shaped by nationalist political groups which agitated for independence, both peacefully, and by way of insurgency. Given these similarities, comparing and contrasting the methods of interdiction used in these conflicts is a logical next step. However, despite this seemingly similar setup, the conflicts differed in two very important respects:

1.  **The two groups differed greatly in their underlying political rationale for counterinsurgency.**

The apartheid republics straightforwardly fought to keep political power concentrated within their white minorities. This was enabled by the fact that at the time of their respective insurgencies, the local white populations had already declared independence from the British empire, who had been actively pushing for decolonization and rule by local black elites. By comparison, the French departments of Algeria were still closely integrated with the political establishment of mainland France. As such, while the European settlers (who advocated for white-rule) did exercise a veto in practical terms, they didn't have an unconditional monopoly on how French governments decided their Algerian Policy. These settlers constituted something like a vocal, willing, and well-organized minority which could shout down those in mainland France who, while maybe opposed to the war, generally saw the issue as peripheral when compared to domestic issues.

The political role of the Pied-Noir was largely enabled by the unstable distribution of power in mainland France during the Fourth Republic. Specifically, the system of proportional representation had an electoral barrier to legislative participation which averaged percentages in the single-digits nationally. This led to a proliferation of parties which were faced with issues that didn’t map cleanly onto the left-right axis. This combination of problems led to case-by-case coalitions which were, by necessity, temporary. Any equilibria established to address a specific crisis would collapse by the time the next had come around. Since no group had a solid, lasting political majority, the cohesive mass of Pied-Noir voters functioned as a sizable political force.

In short, there were those, generally on the center or left in mainland France, who advocated complete decolonization or otherwise for an independent Algeria closely associated with France. There were those, generally European settlers in Algeria or of the right wing in mainland France, who could abide nothing but the unabashed maintenance of “l'Algérie française” [15]. There were those, generally of the French Army or Gaullist political sphere, who wanted to preserve the integrity of the French departments, but to do so while integrating the French and North African populations. Lastly, there was the view of Charles de Gaulle himself: that by attempting to swallow the French departments of Algeria whole, France would choke to death before digestion ever set in. As one can probably infer, this overlapping web of views seriously complicates any attempt to determine a single political “goal” of French policy on Algérie:

> > The army was soon to summarize its vast program in one simple phrase, "L'Algérie française," and in one word, "integration". However, it would be a mistake to think that the officers using this word accorded it an extremely precise institutional meaning. A great diversity of opinion existed in military circles about the nature of future administrative ties which ought to unite Algerian and metropolitan departments. Some were in favor of complete assimilation, while others were aware of an Algerian "personality" endowed with its own institutions. In fact, as it was used by the army until May 13, 1958, integration essentially corresponded to a synthesis of three major elements: the fundamental imperatives of military policy in Algeria, the maintenance of French sovereignty, and the attainment by the Moslems of full civil equality, and economic and social progress.
>
> [...]
>
> > Understood in this sense, integration constituted the idea-force, the myth that the army intended to offer to the dreams of the Algerian masses and oppose the mystique of independence. "Our myth,” declares Colonel Lacheroy, "involves telling the Moslem, you will be like us." This may lead to a misconception. It was not just cold calculation or a simple propaganda maneuver, but a profound desire by the army to achieve an ideal condition. This condition involved the complete abrogation of semicolonial statutes existing in Algeria until 1954, and the establishment of entirely new ties between Algerian and metropolitan departments. At this point, the military were quite far from their original task of re-establishing order. The army had set up a goal in the Algerian fight that, without expressly contradicting it, greatly transcended the goal sought by the government [16].

The position on Algeria held by the French army was due as much to a wellspring of idealism as an acute sense of cynicism. If irregular warfare is conflict over populations rather than the land on which they reside, it must also be a war over what the population believes. And in a war of ideas, narratives are weapons of mass destruction. Any counterinsurgent, even one seeking more or less to maintain the status quo, must approach this task from a subversive direction and with revolutionary zeal. It is true that “integration” was developed by the French Army because they actively sought a “system of values strong enough to unite and stimulate national energies” [17]. It was the narrative they sought to use to inoculate the Algerians against Algerian nationalism and independence. At the same time, it would not be correct to say that this meant it was concocted as a lie. Genuine hope was invested in it, as well as deliberate myopia. It reflected the guilty conscience of a relatively liberal society who felt that they had been “forced” into heavy-handed repression. It represented the universal wish of all the empires to be redeemed by the “civilizing influence” of their dominion.

It should also be noted that the psychology of the French army in Algeria was all but dominated by the traumatic experiences of that generation. The fall of Metropolitan France and the Armistice of 1940. The stain of occupation and collaboration with the Axis. Their decisive, crushing defeat in Indochina and more so, the Vietnamese who they had worked with and left behind [18]. This siege mentality would only metastasize as the war went on with the abandonment of Morocco, which the army had seen as its favored Lyauteyan protectorate, and of Tunisia thereafter; and finally by the humiliating withdrawal from the Suez Canal which the United States and Soviet Union had forced upon the joint Franco-British-Israeli force despite its military success. The French Departments of Algeria formed the final bastion of French honor in what Raoul Girardet called the “sentimental geography” of the French Army [19]. In the face of all this, the Algerian war had simply become a war that could not be lost [20].

2. **Aside from underlying political differences, the second reason the conflicts differed is in the exact methods used.**

The French army, unlike the security forces of the apartheid republics, favored an approach heavily influenced by the theory of "Revolutionary Warfare", or <em>Guerre Révolutionnaire</em> [21]. In effect, this approach contextualized their military methods using psychological and political warfare rather than a strictly conventional view. The natural consequence of this doctrinal difference between the French and the apartheid republics, as well as their differing political goals, meant that both the reason for and methods of interdiction had a number of appreciable differences. For the South African SADF, their goal in Angola was to interdict insurgents seeking South West Africa by diversion and disruption with surrogate forces. For the Rhodesian RSF, their goal was to interdict insurgents by annihilating adversary infrastructure in sanctuary states via combined-arms border incursions. Both of these groups, however, saw interdiction as the center of their strategy. By comparison, the French Army used static interdiction along the Algerian borders to enable internal pacification. This approach, like South Africa’s, sought to interdict by diversion and disruption. It differed, however, in the sense that their interdiction effort was not the strategic goal in and of itself. Instead, it was a tangential component of the political war.

This key emphasis, that of strategic focus, was highlighted in a 1957 briefing General Jacques Allard gave to the Supreme Headquarters, Allied Powers Europe (SHAPE). In his speech, Allard characterized French COIN doctrine as being composed of two main elements: Construction and Destruction. By construction, he referred to the acts of psychological warfare, political warfare, reconstruction, and nation-building needed to delegitimize the foundations of the insurgency. Likewise by destruction, Allard explained:

> > [It meant] first of all to uncover, dismantle, and suppress the rebel politico-administrative framework, the nerve center of the rebellion. Through it, the mass of the population – willingly or more usually by force – [will be] taken in hand, separated into groups, indoctrinated, pressured, terrorized – caught by one means or another in a tightly meshed net from which there is no escape.
>
> [...]
>
> > Destruction further means to chase and annihilate the armed enemy bands, that is to say, the guerrilla units, which do not form the rebellion's nerve center but one of its means of action. The guerrillas are the fists of the rebellion; their task is to deal spectacular blows against the legal forces (ambush is their favorite tactic), to maintain insecurity, to demonstrate the rebellion's power to the people by constant threats and frequent actions, by bloody exactions, and to fake the impression of a whole army. This mission to destroy the bands, essentially military in character, assumes the form of anti-guerrilla operations with all the difficulties this implies in a country that consists largely of rugged, mountainous terrain, often thickly covered with forest. It requires a great deal of manpower, much of it scattered over the countryside, in combined units (with air support) but containing a heavy preponderance of infantry [22].

Therefore reduced to their constituent terms, Allard divided the destructive element of counterinsurgency into three interrelated tasks: interdiction, degradation, and decapitation. (1) to control where and when the enemy acts, (2) to use attrition to minimize the adversary’s future destructive potential, and (3) to neutralize the adversary’s OPA. 

Despite the semantic order in the statement above, there is no inherent sequential flow to the application of these tasks. French efforts at military interdiction were applied simultaneously with political operations aimed at persuasion and police actions aimed at degradation or decapitation. Each element therefore represented a flexible, overlapping, and mutually-supporting section of a single layered strategy. Nevertheless, it can be said that Interdiction exercised disproportionate influence because without it, the FLN could continuously regenerate and infiltrate units back into French Algeria. That is why a delicate balance was needed. **Without Interdiction, any attempt to attack the adversary directly couldn’t be decisive. However, without attacking the adversary, interdiction alone couldn’t end the insurgency**.

## The Algerian Insurgency

For conflicts which are irregular in nature, the sequencing of their escalation is linked to recurring issues with their historiography. Generally speaking, the average level of violence is higher in conventional interstate wars. As a result, these conflicts tend to have a clearly delineated start and a formal termination of hostilities. The escalation isn’t gradual and continuous, but rather, it is generally immediate and discrete. One second, two countries are at peace. By the next, they are at open war. While the mean level of violence is higher, the overall duration is shorter. 

Irregular conflicts differ in the sense that they often simmer for long periods as the level of violence escalates to warfare. Before the conflict is ever identified, much less named, low-level violence has generally existed for years if not decades. The natural consequence is that irregular wars lack an easily identifiable day when the conflict began. Instead, the day identified as the start of hostilities is usually just an escalation after which it is impossible for the “uninvolved” population to ignore. Specifically for Algeria, this model captures the dynamics of their insurgency with precision.

In the colonial period of Algerian history following the 1830 occupation of Algiers, the North African population organized several resistance movements against the French. Most famously, the Jihad of Emir Abd el-Kader. Even with the suppression of widespread organized resistance by the 1850s, French punitive campaigns would continue throughout the turn of the century due to the sheer number of spontaneous revolts. With bloodshed and time, however, the situation in Algeria calmed and the French presence was normalized. This was largely seen as the successful conclusion of large-scale military pacification. It was not until decades later, with the end of the Second World War, that resistance against France would be seriously reignited.

On May 8, 1945, representatives of the German government signed the Instrument of Surrender, bringing the European Front of WWII to its legal conclusion. In cities across the Allied Powers, citizens broke out into impromptu “Victory Day” celebrations; in France, this was no different. However on the same day, thousands of kilometers to the south, Victory Day celebrations in the North African cities of Sétif and Guelma merged with Algerian demonstrations for self-determination. The colonial gendarmerie reacted quickly to suppress the demonstrators, a move which backfired and led to tragedy. The exact truth of what happened that day has been lost to history, but by its conclusion, both native Algerians and French police had been killed. These deaths galvanized Algerian nationalists into riots where they formed mobs roaming the countryside and targeting European settlers. These settlers formed militias of their own and worked with local gendarmerie to conduct reprisals.

By the time the colonial government had gotten control over the situation, hundreds of Europeans and thousands of Native Algerians had been massacred. Strictly speaking, asking who is “to blame” for the deaths is the wrong question. What matters more to the historiography of the Algerian War is that this exchange served as a point of no return. It is from this point that the fundamental occurrence of the Algerian war was rendered inevitable. At the same time, however, it is hard to set these massacres as the start of the conflict because they lacked what the National Liberation Front [Front de Libération Nationale, FLN] brought to the conflict: a single, overarching faction to represent the Algerian Independence. It is for this reason that the string of terrorist attacks which occurred almost a decade later, on All Saints’ Day, 1954, are more commonly used to mark the start of the conflict.

The “Toussaint Rouge” [Red All Saints’ Day] differed from the preceding escalations in the fact that it was an organized paroxysm of terrorist violence which targeted the foundations of French rule. While the prior revolts were overwhelmingly spontaneous, this functioned as a targeted declaration of war. More so, it allowed the newly-formed FLN to indicate its sole position of leadership in the struggle for independence [23]. From late 1954, the scale and intensity of violence would only continue to grow but it did so in direct proportion to the size, influence, and power of the FLN. Specifically, the escalation would largely follow the tit-for-tat pattern set forth in Sétif. By targeting European settlers, the FLN could goad the Pied-Noirs into “ratonnades” [rat-hunts] against the native population. The native population, caught in the middle, had no choice but to choose a side. Politically speaking, this cycle was autocatalytic. The chain of progressive escalation radicalized both the North African and European populations into polarized opposing factions. Faced with this runaway explosion of civil violence, the government could not stay passive. The number of French soldiers in Algeria would steadily climb from the start of unrest, reaching around 200,000 during the governor-generalship of Jacques Soustelle before almost doubling with the appointment of Robert Lacoste. 

This was almost eight times the number of garrison troops in Algeria at the time of the All Saints’ Day, 1954 [24]. But the fact remains that these infusions of manpower did little to alleviate pressure in the short-term. For one, the “bouclage” [encirclement] and “ratissage” [search-and-destroy] operations conducted by the French forces in the early war were slow and clumsy. They set out to find and annihilate insurgent units but categorically failed to check the expansion of the FLN. The concentration of large French units could only occur by relocating forces from other areas, leaving them vulnerable to guerillas [25]. Plus, the slow assembly of men couldn’t be hidden, removing surprise as an advantage. What limited military success the French found in this period, failed to stop the FLN from steadily spreading its political net to include the Algerian populations of the cities and “the bled” [the Algerian outback] alike. By admission of official French reports, the period from 1954 to 1956 can be described as one in which **“France could not stop the spread of the revolt, and the FLN spread its influence over the entire country” [26].**

Notably, 1956 was also the year in which Tunisia achieved victory in their relatively bloodless struggle for independence against France. By the time 1957 began and the Algerian war entered its most infamous phase (the Battle of Algiers), the French departments of Algeria had been completely encircled by nations which were neutral at best and hostile at worst to the concept of its very existence. These icy relations between France and the nations bordering Algeria would prove to be one of the key boons of the insurgency. Their hostility made them willing to act as sanctuaries and the FLN used these states to host all types of critical facilities: political headquarters, command posts, depots, logistical offices, workshops, hospitals, training centers, radio stations, and more [27]. This dispersion across Morocco, Tunisia, and to a lesser degree, Libya, was so widespread that it would go on to shape the organizational form of the FLN itself. Almost more than any other internal divisions, the split between the “interior” and “exterior” delegations (that is, those prosecuting the war from inside or outside of Algeria) would dominate intra-FLN politics. At its conception, the FLN’s political bureau, the Revolutionary Committee of Unity and Action [Comité Révolutionnaire d’Unité et d’Action; CRUA], and its later successor the Coordination and Execution Committee [Comité de Coordination et d’Exécution; CCE], would operate out of their headquarters in Algiers. This meant that within the FLN, the interior branches as a whole could monopolize political control over the exterior. However, the crushing French victory in the Battle of Algiers forced the CCE into exile, where they would later form the Provisional Government of the Algerian Republic [Gouvernement Provisoire de la République algérienne; GPRA]. This reversal flipped the status-quo, elevating the status of many external branches from being peripheral to their struggle for independence, to being central to it.

### **Mobility and Ground-Interdiction**

As one could probably infer, the FLN’s military wing, the National Liberation Army [<em>Armée de la Libération Nationale</em>; ALN], was heavily overrepresented among the ranks of the internal delegation. This presence was linked mainly to the several “wilayas” [autonomous military provinces] which the FLN used to organize its operations in Algeria. Each of these zones was directed by a commander, generally a Colonel in the ALN, who had been tasked with recruiting, organizing, and training bands of guerillas for employment against the French [28]. This can be contrasted with the External Delegation, which was largely made up of the political functionaries and the support apparatus of the FLN. While the internal arm of the ALN ran day-to-day operations, the external wing of the FLN was focused on soliciting financial and military assistance, internationalizing the war, seeking foreign diplomatic recognition, and ultimately, setting the political goals of the conflict (and thereby, determining the ALN’s strategic goals) [29]. However, aside from the bulk of ALN forces within Algeria, there existed a growing portion of ALN strength in the exterior. By this point in the conflict, they were chiefly tasked with the infiltration of military forces and foreign weapons back across the border. In effect, they were the key piece ensuring that the resources amassed by the external branch could be transferred back to the interior. By 1957, this smuggling effort had actually become one of the main functions of the ALN as a whole [30].

In spite of the relatively hard line taken by the French in Algeria, their initial interdictive actions weren’t all military in nature. The French Navy played an active role in coast guard operations, intercepting shipments along the Algerian Coast and seizing any with arms destined for the FLN [31]. Over the course of this naval interdiction, the French intercepted ten vessels carrying a combined sum of 1350 tons of matériel [32]. In the same manner, the French diplomatic corps was empowered to exert an extraordinary amount of pressure on nations both hostile and friendly. This had the twin goals of both limiting recognition of the FLN, and dissuading western nations from selling arms to sanctuary nations (As the U.S. and U.K. would come to learn during arms deals with Tunisia) [33]. Unfortunately for the French, dealing arms to the FLN had become profitable enough for freelance and private arms dealers to get involved. While practical necessity had forced the French to exercise restraint in dealing with other sovereign governments, here they could unleash French intelligence (SDECE) to act without restraint. When possible, French intelligence used a delicate mixture of subterfuge and threats to “dissuade” arms dealers from dealing with the FLN. However, there were limits to this approach. In the face of particularly recalcitrant actors, French intelligence was more than willing to simply resort to liquidation [34]. 

Individually, any one of these efforts was useful but considered as a whole, their cumulative effect left much to be desired. Like trying to patch a leaky boat with scotch tape, this piecemeal approach to interdiction just couldn’t deal with the sheer scale of the smuggling. Furthermore, the most significant influx of FLN support came by physically moving matériel across the border. What was needed was a large-scale, systematic approach and André Morice, the newly appointed Minister of Defense, would make this a top French priority [35].

The borders between Algeria and its neighbors, Morocco and Tunisia, are arbitrary. They extend as far inland as the plateau and lack defining topological features like a large river or mountain range. This geography complicates interdiction because any attempt to impede transportation does not have existing features to work with. André Morice decided to face this problem by sponsoring the construction of a network of barrages and defensive reinforcements which would take his name upon completion. Specifically, this network was designed to isolate the ALN troops in-country, from their men, supplies, and leadership in the nearby sanctuaries. In spite of a cursory similarity to the Maginot Line, its conceptual predecessor from the Second World War, the Morice Line had a completely different function, and therefore, a completely different form. The first phase of the Line focused on the roughly 300 kilometers along the Tunisian frontier. By September 1957, a barrier had been constructed along the existing road from Bône to Souk-Ahras, then continued to Tébessa [36]. Later extensions of the Line in 1958 extended it further south to Negrine. This Tunisian section of the line would later be expanded and reinforced by General Challe in 1959. This newer section, commonly called the “Challe Line”, started in La Calle on the Mediterranean coast and doubled the Morice Line to El-Ma-el-Abiod, where the Sahara desert began. A longer, though nowhere near as exhaustive, network had also been developed on the frontier with Morocco. At completion, this eastern barrage spanned around 700 kilometers.

The specific layout of the lines were a function of the terrain, but in general, the Morice Line was centered around a layer of eight-foot fences. In addition, these had been charged with about 5,000 volts of electricity. On either side of this fence lay a hundred meter ring of antipersonnel mines (1.2 million mines in total), which had been encircled in turn by two parallel rows of continuous barbed wire [37]. Directly following the layer of static fortifications ran a string of occupied strongpoints with powerful searchlights. An interesting characteristic of the Morice Line was that, unlike the Maginot line, it was not designed to be impenetrable. The point was to stop the ALN forces from dispersing into the Algerian interior, not to stop them from crossing the border itself. Thus, the operational utility of the Morice Line was found in its ability to (1) degrade ALN forces as they passed across the line, (2) quickly identify where the ALN infiltration parties were, and (3) enable the French to intercept them. This three-step process was enabled by a string of radar-based alarms that had been installed in 25 to 40 mile intervals. The radar sets used on the Morice Line were based on the American AN/MPQ-10. In the Korean war, the U.S. used these radars to track artillery shells and locate their point of origin for counterbattery fire, but on the Morice Line, they were used instead for moving target indication [38]. The flat, empty terrain of the Tunisian frontier was suboptimal for fortification, but for radar performance, it proved to be about as optimal as one could expect. In effect, FLN infiltration parties had no way of passing over the barrages without being detected. On the other hand, the high rate of false alarms proved to be a significant drawback of the system [39]. While all guerillas were detected, it was impossible for the radars to differentiate between an insurgent and an antelope.

While this high rate of false positives made it difficult to identify infiltration by radar, any information it gave could be substantiated, and thereby processed, by additional methods. The system retained its core utility because the radars were never used in isolation. The electrification of the Morice Line’s fences were enabled by a string of power transmission plants every fifteen to twenty kilometers [40]. These transmission stations could be used by the French Army to determine if the fences had been cut, and at which precise point along the line this cut had occurred [41]. This information could be used to dispatch quick reaction forces (QRFs) by rail, road, or even helicopter. If for any reason, this was insufficient, a number of fighters, bombers, maritime patrol, and light observation planes were used to maintain 24/7 visual and aerial surveillance over the line. These aircraft could be used as an alternative method of verification.

When an infiltration alarm had been verified by secondary methods, such as visual confirmation or a cut in the fence, that information could be passed to a 105-mm artillery battery or to the armed patrols which had been routed to preassigned locations on the initial alert. The first, and most difficult, task of the border troops was to make contact with the insurgents. Once this had been done, pinning down and liquidating the offending party was more routine. The important thing, from the point of the view of the defenders, was to ensure that the insurgents could not disperse into the interior. Aside from the sector forces permanently assigned to the line, the elite core of the French Army was held in reserve: the Foreign Legion, parachutists, marines, and mechanized infantry. It was primarily the job of these reserves to react to breaches in the line and encircle the infiltrators.

When compared to the “standard” costs of suppressing an insurgency, the Morice Line was far from inexpensive. However, when weighed directly against annual defense expenditures during the war, they turn out to be relatively negligible. Colonel René Laure, a French brigade commander during the war, approximated the cost of the line at 5 million old francs per kilometer (12,500 USD at the time) [42]. Though, this was just a rough baseline. The real cost of the line was a function of the devices attached or forgone in a given segment. It’s hard to give an exact number, but the total cost of the Morice Line was likely on the order of billions of old francs, or a couple million USD in the period. Contextualized with annual French defense expenditures of the war, this was a trivial percentage. Somewhere around 0.6% of the Army budget, or 0.2% of all defense spending [43].

For comparison, one can look at the costs of its aforementioned predecessor, the Maginot Line, which were practically exorbitant. The fortified regions completed by 1935 had cost the government around 5 to 7 billion FF, but this number is somewhat misleading due to inflation [44]. At the time, this price represented somewhere between 100 to 300 million USD. While still somewhat variable, this represented a considerably larger, between 2% to 5%, share of annual defense expenditures [45]. Using 2025 USD for direct comparison, the Morice Line cost tens of millions of dollars, while the Maginot Line cost billions. Or, more simply put, the Morice Line cost roughly one-tenth to one-twentieth the cost of the Maginot Line while covering a region two to three times the total length.

With this in mind, it is apparent that the financial strain of the interdictive barriers were quite tolerable for France. Their real price was in the manpower requirements needed to keep them operational. Just for maintenance alone, it required almost three thousand men from the Service du Matériel. To man the line and stop infiltration, a much higher number of soldiers were required. In total, around 80,000 soldiers were positioned along the barrages with a full half of this number going to manning the western line [46]. This was a noticeably expensive use of men because the units were tied down in “static” operations. Each man sent to the line was a man taken from the mobile reserves, which was the truly decisive arm of French counterinsurgency.

In this sense, the Morice Line had the same inherent weaknesses as "quadrillage", the French program which imposed territorial control with a network of garrisons and fortified posts. Taking a purely military perspective, the system lent a measure of “permanent equilibrium” which could not be matched by the temporary but more decisive operations in the bled. In addition, garrisons did succeed in undermining insurgent concentration of force in Algiers, and later, non-Saharan Algeria as a whole. However as alluded to, this came with significant manpower costs. There was an almost ten to one difference in manpower, with a single man in the Reserve Générale for ten in a territorial garrison.

This period of the conflict, commonly referred to as the “Battle of the Barrages” or the “Battle of the Borders”, began with a sharp intensification of infiltration attempts. The ALN attempted to cross in periods of bad weather or darkness primarily using speed and stealth. As this became harder, nationalist forces increasingly relied on executing diversionary attacks at multiple points of the line. Meanwhile, at the actual point of infiltration, an assault group would attempt to create and hold a beachhead long enough for transport groups to cross [47]. Their approach yielded some initial success, but this proved to be associated less with the tactics of the ALN as much as the French still perfecting the coordination of units and systems along the Morice Line.

### **The Battle of the Borders**

The immediate FLN response to the Morice Line was to try and break it. This would prove to be one of their costliest mistakes of the war. It is, however, straightforward to see the rationale behind the decision. The entire strategy of the ALN was dependent on the ability to maintain the lines of communication between the interior and the exterior. Likewise, their relatively high tempo of operations was dependent on external rear areas where troops could rest, train, and be re-equipped without threat of harm. Around the winter of 1957, the time of the line’s completion, the ALN had roughly ten thousand men (armed or in-training) positioned along the frontier zone in Tunisia [48]. In addition, tens of thousands of weapons had been concentrated in the Tunisian frontier for transport into Algeria. If these men and matériel couldn’t cross into the interior, and continue to do so at a relatively constant rate, their forces in the wilayas would be defanged by sheer scale of attrition.

Considering the relatively conventional nature of this phase, it isn’t surprising that losses were significant for both the FLN and France. However, by maintaining the integrity of the line, the French prevented ALN regeneration **within** Algeria. This meant that the losses taken by the ALN were comparatively more drastic. In hindsight, both the infiltration attempts and the larger scale battalion-size assaults on the Morice Line resulted in decisive military success for the French. Efforts were made to skirt the Morice Line by sending convoys south through the Sahara desert, but even if successful (which it generally wasn’t), this could have never matched the flow of logistics from the northern crossings. By 1958 to 1959, the military balance had been tipped decisively in the direction of the French:

> In the cities terrorism had been defeated; in the bled [FLN] military successes were few and far between, and morale was down; on the frontiers there was costly stagnation. It continued to be a time of setback and failure within Algeria, but of greater success abroad—and, with historic consequences, the former was the fact most tantalizingly visible to the French army commanders on the spot. Here was the basic contrast: France was strong, militarily, in Algeria, but weak, politically, at home; the F.L.N. was weak, militarily, at home, but strong politically, abroad [49].

### **An Evaluation of French Interdiction**

In his analysis of revolutionary conflicts, Mao Zedong identified a specific model of IW (the "Protracted People's War") in which a rebel group transitions to a professional army by passing through three main stages [50]. 

1. At their inception, the revolutionary movement begins with a period of **initial growth, preparation, and mobilization**.

This phase sees the political grievances underlying the conflict drive an affected population into the hands of the insurgent as clandestine ties develop. Generally, this begins in remote areas and hinterlands far from state power. The insurgent may have military potential at this point, but their primary goal is twofold: to spread the roots of their OPA as a parallel hierarchy and to preserve their potential for future action. As such, they avoid the risk of direct contact and conduct military operations in a piecemeal fashion. It is only once the organization achieves critical mass and controls the necessary plurality of the active population, that the second phase begins.

2. Having completed the necessary organizational development, they begin to use **guerilla tactics** to enable **politico-military consolidation**. 

Here the insurgent continues to expand, but does so in a manner which is more aggressive in nature. The fundamental goal is to break the state’s monopoly of violence and undermine the basis of their civil order. Sections of the general population which cannot be swayed are coerced. Terrorism, rather than occurring in a piecemeal fashion, is consistent and systematic. Organized violence is directed at symbols for, instruments of, or social elements loyal to state power. Simultaneously, matériel is smuggled, stolen, or otherwise acquired to aid the expansion of military forces. While still fundamentally seeking to avoid military contact, this phase sees the insurgent recalibrate his tolerance in order to accept higher risks in pursuit of an expanded set of goals. 

3. When the adversary is sufficiently weakened and the insurgent is sufficiently strong, the people's war enters its final phase: **conventionalization**. 

At this point the guerilla units transform into a standing military force and the insurgent seeks decision through direct military contact. Using conventional strategy, pitched battles, and maneuver warfare the revolutionary force seeks to create political conditions whereby they can achieve a favorable end to the war. Often negotiation is used at this point, but it is generally not as a good-faith attempt, but as an additional form of political warfare.

Roughly speaking, this three-stage process was used by the Chinese Communists against the KMT, the Viet Minh against France, and to a lesser degree, the Viet Cong against the United States. It was also the loose strategic framework used by the FLN in Algeria [51]. However, unlike with Indochina, the French had learned a costly lesson. They would not let the Algerians move freely from one phase of revolution to the next. With the completion of the Morice Line, the FLN forces in Algeria were well into the second phase of revolution and were actively preparing for progression to the third. <b>This ended promptly with the vigorous French defense of the Tunisian frontier.</b> From then on, the ALN troops of the interior were trapped in the wilayas and the FLN resources in the exterior were trapped in the sanctuaries. 

As is often observed in irregular warfare, the heights of military success can coincide with the depths of political failure. The Algerian War is no exception. Just as the French Army had ramped up the efficiency of their “cordon sanitaire”, the ascension of Pierre Pflimlin as Prime Minister led the army to mutiny over fears of a negotiated settlement. This crisis of May 1958 would lead both to the collapse of the Fourth Republic and return of Charles de Gaulle. Having been brought back into power to appease the revolting Army, de Gaulle set forth to establish some measure of order over the conflict. To this end, he transferred Raoul Salan out of Algeria, dividing his plenipotentiary powers in Algeria into two separate civil and military leaders. Namely, the Air Force General, Maurice Challe, and the technocrat, Paul Delouvrier. It had become obvious that with the success of the Morice Line, ALN fighters had been effectively trapped. General Challe sought to finally deal with these units with a series of operations known as the “Challe Plan”.

Combat operations associated with the plan began in 1959 and lasted roughly a year in total. Since by this point, the FLN had already been driven out of the cities, the plan focused on the mountainous stretches covering much of northern Algeria. As FLN strongholds were identified, large portions of the Réserve Générale would descend upon the region and block off routes of escape. French units would then comb through the encircled area, finding and liquidating guerilla bands. As a mountain range was cleared, control would shift from the Réserve Générale to harkis (native Algerian auxiliary units). If the French operations of this period could be characterized by just two key attributes, it would be the exertion of unceasing pressure and making a veritable fetish of mobility. At their core, these operations resembled standard search-and-destroy missions, but could be differentiated by their significantly extended duration and much more specific intelligence [52]. When the French units arrived in an area, they could be sure of the insurgent presence and thus were willing to stay as long as it took.

The various offensives began in zones of least resistance and progressively moved in the direction of FLN strongholds. This gradual advance peaked with the eight-month “Operation Jumelles” in Wilaya III, which was said to house approximately thirteen to fourteen Katibas (~11,000 fighters) [53]. By April 1960, Challe had made the single greatest French effort to annihilate ALN forces within the interior. The nationalist forces made the regeneration of forces a core priority, but without access to matériel and rear areas to train, these replacements could not be organized into coherent military units. This problem was further exacerbated by the decimation of ALN leadership during the offensives. From the beginning of the Challe plan up to April 1960, a total of five wilaya commanders had been killed [54].

Like with any irregular conflict, conducting accurate assessments of a nonstate actor’s manpower reserves is nigh-impossible. What is easier, on the other hand, is measuring trends and changes. As a direct result of the Morice Line and the Challe Plan, the Algerian nationalists suffered losses which were undoubtable serious. These losses would have a disastrous effect on their military effectiveness. Due chiefly to a lack of manpower, ALN units within Algeria had been forced to downsize their fundamental combat units from the Battalion (Fai'leks) into the Company (Katiba) [55]. At times, these companies had to be further broken up into Platoons (Ferkas) or groups of ten to fifteen men, a new formation called the “commando.” With further attrition, this trend of disintegration would only continue. At the start of Challe’s operations, the FLN had gone from small groups conducting sabotage and assassination, to organized company-size operations with crew-served weapons. By the end of it, they were working with, <em>at best</em>, understrength platoons [56].

The story implied by this military disintegration is straightforward: the Morice Line was able to interdict by disruption, and subsequently, diversion. The cumulative effect of isolating and destroying the rebel units in-country was that the French prevented the FLN from successfully completing the transition to the third phase of revolutionary warfare. It is very important to note, however, that a failure to conventionalize the war did not mean that ALN failed to develop a standing army. Over the disastrous years of 1958 to 1959, power in the FLN became increasingly centralized under the figure of Houari Boumedienne, who was tasked with restoring discipline to the ALN [57]. Principally, this meant halting the frontal offensives on the Morice Line. While this course of action did allow Boumedienne to restore a semblance of morale to the interior, it also meant that the FLN had ceded a large portion of their freedom of action. With no way to send men across the borders, the nationalists now had thousands of soldiers stranded in Tunisia and Morocco.

Rather than issuing a general demobilization, these men were reorganized and retrained along conventional lines and armed with heavy weapons. This standing army, the “Corps de Bataille” (Battle Corps), would become the base of support later used by Boumedienne to dominate the FLN and monopolize political power after the revolution. Until the day it invaded Algeria in force, the conventional ALN forces could never play a militarily active role. Yet, somewhat contradictorily, having an “Army-in-being” played to the strengths of the FLN. As long as this conventional force was co-located with the GPRA in Tunisia, it lent the government-in-exile some measure of diplomatic weight, prestige, and above all, permanency. This was crucial to negotiating a favorable end to the conflict with France. By conducting an effective interdiction campaign, the French had created a conventional threat in a territory where they couldn't intervene.

The historian Peter Paret asserts that the Morice line was “a passive measure, and therefore, inadequate.” This statement is misleading. Paret is correct insofar as the barriers were static and could never address the rebels located in exterior sanctuaries. However, the purpose of the Morice Line was not to “win” the war for the French. What Paret has done here, is conflate the results of the war with the results of interdiction. The line was created to prevent the adversary from effectively using its forces to achieve military objectives. It did this successfully.

> **Lt. General Yves P. Ezanno, French Air Force:**
>
> > From early 1959 until the end of the war, no enemy formation penetrated the fences, but occasionally one or two men were able to get through. [...] Nobody saw a Katiba any more, or anything larger than a platoon. The rebel forces were broken. Both fences, as I said, were real tight. Nobody went through. The war was over in Algeria as far as military action was concerned. I am not talking about the political side. I am talking about the military aspect.

As a direct result of the Morice Line, the ALN’s forces inside of Algeria withered. The subsequent mop-up operations of General Challe took advantage of this to deliver the coup de grâce. The forces that did remain in the wilayas, mainly fragmented bands, were forced to revert back to smaller-scale guerilla warfare and terrorism. This lower scale and tempo of operations gave them time and space to lick their wounds. However, at least from an orthodox perspective, this reversion to more indirect methods meant that the FLN had failed to transition from the second phase (guerrilla struggle) to the third (conventional warfare), a step supposedly required for victory in Maoist theory. This was something which the French officer corps was undoubtedly aware of.

Having experienced defeat at the hands of the Viet Minh and even, in some cases, spending months in reeducation camps, a section of French officers developed somewhat of an obsession with the writings of Mao and Lenin. This is not to say that they became political leftists, but rather, that they felt a burning desire to understand why they had lost so decisively and to learn from it. Colonel Trinquier went as far as to describe the advocates of “psychological action” in the French Army as “Communist[s] without doctrine” [59]. In aiming to win a revolutionary war from the counterinsurgent perspective, they accepted the necessity of a sort of military-Leninism, limited only to methods. As Jean-Marie Domenach puts it:

> both in method and in strategy the activist section of the French Army entered into a sort of dialectical collaboration with the Communist enemy.

Given this context, it is reasonable to assume that there were those in the French Army who knew the FLN sought to conventionalize the war and saw the Morice Line as a direct method to prevent this. From the historical perspective, we know that the Algerian War, along with many of the African bush wars (Mau-Mau rebellion, South African Border War, Rhodesian Bush War), proved to be noticeable exceptions to this rule. Victory was achieved in these conflicts without ever having to conventionalize the conflict. Yet this is information which we know with hindsight.

### **Political Collapse and the Limits of Interdiction**

With the Morice Line and Challe Plan, France had gained the initiative, and therein, the potential to direct the conflict. Notably, the operative word here is potential. The fact that interdiction gave France a political opportunity does not mean they would, or could, make use of it. The conclusion of the Challe Plan, which largely spelled the end of open-warfare in Algeria, also happened to coincide with a peak of public disillusionment in France. They were so close to peace, yet frustratingly far.

For the French state, and by follow-on, the French Army, this lack of willpower and public support was their center of gravity. Quite appropriately then, it was here where the FLN had ultimately directed their efforts. At the time of the Toussaint Rouge, suppression of the FLN was such a consensus position that even the French left wing supported it. Before the Battle of Algiers, it was Robert Lacoste, a socialist, who delegated power to General Massu. By the late war, for comparison, the political context had shifted so much that there were even those on the French right who saw the war as futile. The most consequential shift for the French, however, occurred not among the general public, but in the mind of Charles de Gaulle.

With the collapse of the Fourth Republic, de Gaulle had been brought into power largely to prevent a general revolt of the Army. The French officer corps had feared their government would “sell out” Algeria in a political deal. Like they believed had been done with Indochina, Morocco, and Tunisia - among others. Reading a little between the lines here, the Army allowed de Gaulle to take power because they believed he would preserve the integrity of “Algérie française.” What was unknown both to those who had and hadn’t blessed his ascension, was that de Gaulle had no intention to do this.

It is hard to say exactly when this shift happened, but for him, it had become clear that costs of the war had far outstripped anything meaningful from which it could be gained. France could bear the financial cost. It might even have been willing to bear the cost in lives. But it could not bear the political cost. Too much blood had been shed for the Algerians to be meaningfully integrated en-masse. There had been too much radicalization for any moderate Algerian organization, such as the Democratic Union of the Algerian Manifesto [Union Démocratique du Manifeste Algérien; UDMA], to exist as a coherent political third force, much less as an avenue for Algerian self-determination on terms agreeable to the French. “Totality is not unity. The state of siege, even when it is extended to the very boundaries of the earth, is not reconciliation” [60]. By prosecuting the war, France had entrenched the FLN. Now, the French were not fighting to win, they were fighting to find an opportunity to leave. This goal, that of escape, meant that the French could never find victory.

The political instability of the Fourth and Fifth Republics take on a darker shade when one considers the multiple instances of military rebellion. These did not necessarily represent the Army taking a revolutionary, nor even “active”, role against the state. After all, unlike the Spanish-speaking world, juntas, caudillos, and pronunciamentos, are not in the French Tradition [61]. Instead, this insubordination reflected that the French state had been prosecuting the war under implicit coercion from the army. Formally speaking, the 1958 crisis which brought down the Fourth Republic was not an episode of coup d'état. In a coup, plotters do not issue public calls for new leadership or petition the government for changes in policy. In a coup, the plotters act directly to topple the government. What the 1958 crisis was, is a political ultimatum under the threat of coup d'état. This was a crucial distinction.

The Army had placed itself between the revolting Pied-Noir, the Algerian nationalists, and the French government. In lieu of an effective, or consistent, state response to the Algerian Insurgency, it had usurped the state’s role of arbitration. It had sought to moderate the Pied-Noir revolt and orient it towards “integration”, but it did so by considerably overstepping its bounds.

> In essence, then, the political role of the Army remains negative: it is an invisible pressure, a permanent blackmail, exercised in the name of the superior interests of France and of the West. No longer does the Army tell the State what it must do but what it must not do. It fixes the limits beyond which it will not feel itself bound to obey. These are, first, if French sovereignty in Algeria were abandoned; second, if a Popular Front government were formed in France [62].

The original catalyst for French repression of the FLN was the natural reaction of any state to preserve its monopoly on violence. It wasn’t reflective of any thought process or specific considerations. It was a knee-jerk response. With the passage of time, the reactive measures became “sticky.” That is, incoming governments didn't have the political capital or willpower to change them so they became lasting measures by default. The state felt compelled to continue their course of action, simply out of a sense of inertia. Any attempt to make conscious policy, specifically, that of extricating itself from the situation in which they were trapped, invited the army to revolt. Thus “the Army took on the shape of an autonomous power”, but not one which was independent of the government. The army had instead transformed the French Fourth Republic into a kind of indirect suzerainty. This subversion of authority “was not in order to support a political party or the aspirations of a dictator, but [so] that [the Army] could remain faithful to its mission” [63]. It was sidestepping the will of the government, in order to save the nation “from itself.”

The French army of the period plays a particularly interesting role because while it had an activist wing with many individual revolutionaries, the institution was, as a whole, surprisingly passive. The existence of this insubordinate streak is all but natural considering de Gaulle’s break with the Vichy Government. In purely legal terms, the Vichy regime was the rightful successor of the Third Republic following the 1940 Armistice. Yet, his defection to create a “Free French” government-in-exile was crucial to the myth of salvation among the French people. It had created a precedent which explicitly lionized disobedience in the face of an illegitimate government. This precedent was only made worse by the infamous level of political instability in the Fourth Republic. Their rate of turnover which was so high, that it precluded any consistent approach towards the conflict. When one government took a hardline, another sought to negotiate. This vacillation undermined the pacification efforts of the army and all but forced them to exert pressure in favor of a single consistent approach.

The unique passivity of insubordination in the French Army is best seen in contrast to the Imperial Japanese Army (IJA), which was another organization with an endemic tendency for rebellion. For almost the breadth of its existence, acts of rebellion known as Gekokujō, “the retainer supplanting the lord”, had been more than just normalized in the Japanese Empire. They had been validated. These were supposedly not acts motivated by personal ambition, but attempts to rectify perceived injustices with paramilitary force and assassination [64]. The “honorable” intentions behind Gekokujō socially legitimized the criminal insubordination, allowing plotters to expect minimal consequences in failure. It was exactly this validation of the tradition which incentivized Meiji, Taishō, and especially Shōwa-era officers to organize acts of political revolt. Not just because they worked, but above all, because they could get away with it.

> > The 1930s gave rise to a particularly chaotic form of disobedience. In 1931, a military terror organization named the Sakura-kai (Cherry Blossom Society) attempted to annihilate the entire Japanese cabinet with an air strike. A few months later, rebellious officers assassinated Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi. The ensuing coup d’état failed but led to a series of further violent upheavals, both inside and outside the army. In 1935, a leading general was slashed to death by a junior officer because of a factional rivalry.
>
> [...]
>
> > And finally, in February 1936, state control monetarily collapsed during a massive military insurgency. More than a thousand troops took to the streets, and several Japanese leaders were murdered in their mansions. In a classic book written during the Second World War, the British correspondent Hugh Byas coined the term “government by assassination.” Officers of the 1930s, according to Byas, terrorized the civilian cabinet by way of repeated assassinations. Through their ability to inflict lethal violence, they took control of foreign policy, pushing timid and terrified leaders toward a course of unbridled military expansion [65].

A key difference between French and Japanese military rebellion was that in Japan, conspirators appealed to the imperial personage (namely, the institution of the emperor) rather than the body politic. This was due to the fact that their German-inspired constitution delineated the Army as being beholden to the Emperor, rather than the civil government. Nevertheless, in spite of this de jure authority, the Showa Emperor was politically catatonic. His refusal to intervene enabled the army to disregard the government without fear of punishment or retribution.

This trend of institutionalized disobedience would arguably peak with the Mukden Incident, a false flag operation used by the Kwantung Army to invade and occupy Manchuria. This single event would prove to be the act of disobedience with the most lasting repercussions. As indirect as it may be, with the Mukden Incident, mid-ranking officers were able to drag their nation into the Second Sino-Japanese War, the Pacific War, and World War Two as a whole. This happened completely due to the initiative of these officers, with little to no oversight of public input.

Aside from their varying levels of militancy, this act highlights a central difference between French and Japanese military activism. In the IJA, activists created policy for the state by way of fait accompli, in this way, they functioned as an “active” influence on the state. By contrast, the activists of the French army, sought to prevent the State from changing its existing policy, in this way, they functioned as a “passive” check.

The core of French political failure in the Algerian War was a simple but difficult problem: the French government wanted to leave, while the officer corps wanted to stay. The goal of Integration, which the army had created for itself during the Fourth Republic, was incompatible with the Algerian policy of de Gaulle, who now wished to reassert his dominance. This political breakdown occurred in spite of their military successes. No matter how effective they were, French methods could never made up for the faulty political foundations of the war. And they were effective indeed. Over the course of the conflict, the French achieved something in Algeria which not even the U.S. could do in Vietnam: the successful interdiction of large-scale insurgent logistics. This interdiction was used to successfully reverse the conventionalization of the fighting within Algeria. In essence, you could say the French army was not defeated in Algeria because their interdiction was inadequate. They lost the war because they had no way to convert their military success into political results. This reveals a very important lesson: Successful counterinsurgency requires interdiction, but interdiction does not inherently provide success to counterinsurgency.
